Does La Niña Actually Matter for Colorado Snowpack?

Short answer: no. Knowing whether it is a La Niña year does not tell you if Colorado's snow will be good or bad.

Secondary exploration

The statewide answer hides a north-south split

Colorado sits at the edge of the ENSO teleconnection, with the northern and southern mountains on different sides of it. Splitting the stations into three latitude bands shows a pattern the statewide average cancels out. This is exploratory. None of it reaches significance, and it should be read as a description of the record rather than as a result.

Secondary exploration

El Niño tilts snow south, and La Niña looks like a normal winter

To test the split directly, subtract the southern index from the northern one for each winter, then group those {{stats:provenance.season_count}} differences by ENSO phase. A positive value means the northern mountains ran ahead of the southern mountains that year.

La Niña winters average {{stats:north_south_differential.three_bin.La Nina.mean|2}} points. Neutral winters average {{stats:north_south_differential.three_bin.Neutral.mean|2}}. El Niño winters average {{stats:north_south_differential.three_bin.El Nino.mean|2}}. La Niña and neutral are nearly the same. The tilt belongs to El Niño.

Neither clears the 0.05 threshold. So the geographic tilt is consistent and not significant. Forty-six winters is not enough to separate it from noise. That is the finding, and it is a limit of the record rather than a flaw in the measurement.

Context

The worst snowpack on record came in a neutral winter

The lowest April 1 value in the entire {{stats:provenance.season_count}}-winter record is {{stats:statewide.three_bin.Neutral.min|1}} percent of median, and it belongs to water year {{stats:provenance.resolved_end_wy}}. That winter was ENSO-neutral. If phase drove Colorado snowpack, the record low would not land in the middle category.

One caveat on that season. NRCS reported statewide snowpack falling from about 60 percent of median on March 1, 2026 to roughly 20 percent by April 1, driven by record March warmth and early melt rather than by a failure to accumulate. April 1 snow water equivalent cannot distinguish a winter that never built snowpack from one that built it and lost it early. Both read the same here.

Methodology

Data sources and methods

Stations. The station list was derived, not chosen. A discovery script queried the NRCS AWDB inventory for every Colorado SNOTEL site, then filtered on actual April 1 coverage: a record beginning in water year {{stats:provenance.common_start_wy}} or earlier, continuing to the present, with no more than two missing April 1 values. Region tags were computed from station latitude.

Minimum median filter. Stations whose April 1 median falls below {{stats:provenance.MIN_STATION_MEDIAN_SWE_IN|1}} inches were excluded, because percent of median is unstable at a site where a one-inch difference is a large percentage. This filter removed {{stats:provenance.excluded_station_count_min_median_filter}} stations. This is a judgment call and it is stated here so a reader can disagree with it.

ENSO classification. Water-year phase comes from the NOAA CPC Oceanic Niño Index, averaged across the six overlapping three-month seasons centered on October through March. La Niña is an average at or below {{stats:provenance.oni_thresholds.three_bin.la_nina_max|1}}, El Niño at or above {{stats:provenance.oni_thresholds.three_bin.el_nino_min_inclusive|1}}, neutral in between.

Sample gate. No statistical test is computed on a grouping where any bin holds fewer than {{stats:provenance.min_seasons_for_test}} winters. A five-category split by ENSO strength was computed for description but carries no test, because the strong categories hold {{stats:gate.five_bin.season_counts.Strong La Nina}} and {{stats:gate.five_bin.season_counts.Strong El Nino}} winters.

Reproducibility. Every figure on this page is written into the page by a render script that reads the analysis output. No number is typed by hand. Source: {{stats:provenance.oni_source_url}} and {{stats:provenance.awdb_endpoint}}.

Limitations

What this analysis cannot tell you

  • Several views of the data were examined: statewide, three regions, and a north-south differential. None reached significance. Reporting the largest apparent effect as though it had been the planned test would overstate the evidence.
  • Percent of median is relative to each station's own record, not to the NRCS 1991 to 2020 reference product. Figures here will not match NRCS basin reports exactly.
  • April 1 snow water equivalent measures one date. It does not capture peak timing, total winter precipitation, or melt rate.
  • {{stats:provenance.season_count}} winters is a small sample for comparing three groups. A real effect smaller than roughly ten percentage points would not be detectable here.
